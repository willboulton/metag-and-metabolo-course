## Part 1: Metagenomics Fundamentals

### What is Metagenomics?

**Metagenomics** is the study of genetic material recovered directly from environmental samples, without prior cultivation. Instead of isolating individual organisms, we sequence DNA from a community of microbes, giving us an unbiased snapshot of:

- **Taxonomy**: Which organisms are present and in what abundance?
- **Genes**: What genetic potential does the community possess?
- **Function**: What metabolic pathways are encoded in the community's collective genome?

### The Workflow: From DNA to MAGs, Abundances and Functions

MAGs are metagenome-assembled genomes, i.e. they are putative semi-complete genomes derived from a metagenome using computational methods (sequence assembly, followed by a binning process which clusters together contiguous fragments of DNA from the same organism into a single genome). MAGs are often a core unit in whole genome shotgun microbiome studies, especially for human gut derived samples. The abundances of these MAGs, often measured in terms of reads per million, and the genes called, can then be analysed, and a MAG abundance table might look very similar to any other OTU abundance table, e.g. from 16S studies. 

In the Hildebrand lab, we use a single software pipelines called MATAFILER4 to go from raw reads to abundance tables (plus functional annotation and quite a lot more too). There are many other similar bioinformatics pipelines; most will do very similar steps, often using the same tools. Below is a summary of what we did in the DIME study. After sequencing, all the steps were done using MATAFILER4. 

```
*Environmental Sample*
    ↓
    ↓ (2x faecal samples per participant - 1 per intervention period)
    ↓
*DNA Extraction*
    ↓
    ↓ (Maxwell RSC Purefood Kit - Qubit for quantification)
    ↓
*DNA Sequencing*
    ↓
    ↓ (50 million reads per library, and negative controls, 2x 150 bp NovaSeq 6000)
    ↓
*Quality Control & Assembly* 
    ↓
    ↓ (MATAFILER4: using SDM to remove low-quality reads and MEGAHIT for assembly)
    ↓
Functional Annotation & MAG Recovery
    ↓
    ↓ (Genes called with Prodigal, MAGs binned using SemiBin2, CheckM2 for assessment)
    ↓
Taxonomic Assignment & Abundance Profiling
    ↓
    ↓ (mapping using Strobealign, RTK for abundance profiling, tax assignment using GTDB-Tk) 
    ↓
Biological Interpretation
```

### Key Outputs from Metagenomics

#### 1. **Taxonomic Profiles**
These answer: "Which microorganisms are present?"

- Typically represented as a table with OTUs/ASVs (Operational Taxonomic Units / Amplicon Sequence Variants) as rows and samples as columns
- Contains relative abundance of each taxonomic unit
- Allows comparison of community composition across treatments or time points

#### 2. **Metagenome-Assembled Genomes (MAGs)**
These are **draft genomes reconstructed from community data** by:

1. Assembling short sequencing reads into longer contigs
2. Binning contigs that come from the same organism (based on coverage and tetranucleotide frequency)
3. Dereplicting redundant genomes across samples

**Advantages of MAGs:**
- More complete than single-sample bins
- Allow functional inference at the organism level
- Can be taxonomically classified even if the organism has no cultured representative
- Enable linking of genotypes to phenotypes (phenotypes = metabolites!)

#### 3. **Functional Annotation**
Genes identified in the metagenome are annotated to:

- Functional gene families (KEGG, COG, Pfam, GO terms). 
- Cazymes - using the CAZy database and DIAMOND search. 

This gives us the metabolic potential of the microbial community; we can use these as functional abundance tables as well, but it is also nice to be able to link function to taxonomy through MAGs. 

### Important Considerations

- **Read depth matters**: Rare organisms require deep sequencing to be detected; MAG generation is biased towards abundant organisms that are sometimes better characterised.
- **Assembly matters**: Errors in assembly propagate through to binning and functional annotation.
- **Contaminants**: Eukaryotic or non-target DNA can interfere with analysis.
- **Strain variation**: Based on which samples are assembled together (coassembled), you might lose strain-level information if the assembler is unable to resolve reads from different strains sufficiently well.

## Part 2: Metabolomics Fundamentals

### What is Metabolomics?

**Metabolomics** measures the small molecules (metabolites) present in a sample. These are the products of cellular processes and represent the functional output of biochemistry. In a microbiome context, metabolites include:

- Organic acids (lactate, butyrate, propionate; these last two are short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs))
- Amino acids and derivatives
- Lipids and secondary metabolites
- Host-derived compounds modified by microbes (e.g., polyphenol metabolites)

### Why Metabolomics is Powerful

DNA tells us about the metabolic potential present within bacterial genomes in the microbiome, but metabolomics shows us which compounds are actually present. 

| Aspect | Metagenomics | Metabolomics |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| Detects | Genes (potential) | Actual molecules (function) |
| Specificity | Species/strain level | Often compound-specific |
| Dynamics | Relatively stable | Responsive to conditions |
| Causality | Inferred | Observable |

### Types of Metabolomics

#### **Untargeted Metabolomics**
- Measures as many metabolites as possible in a sample
- Typically uses mass spectrometry (LC-MS in this case, GC-MS is used for other situations involving smaller volotile molecules)
- Produces a large feature matrix (100s to 1000s of features per sample)
- Features must be annotated (matched to known compounds)
- Challenge: Many features are unidentified

#### **Targeted Metabolomics**
- Focuses on a predetermined set of compounds (in our study, SCFAs)
- Higher sensitivity and specificity
- Less comprehensive but more reliable for specific compounds

### Key Metabolomic Outputs

1. **Metabolite abundance table**: Rows = metabolites/features, Columns = samples
2. **Metabolite annotations**: Identity assignments for identified compounds

## Part 3: Integration and Co-Analysis

### Why Integration Matters

When analysing metagenomics and metabolomics separately, you get useful partial pictures:

- Metagenomics: "Species X increased under treatment Y"
- Metabolomics: "Metabolite A increased under treatment Y"

By integrating metagenomic with metabolomic data, we can try to make a more causative link:, e.g. we can ask "Is Species X capable of producing metabolite A?"

This still isn't a slam-dunk (we might like to have RNA transcripts mapping to the pathway producing Metabolite A in Species X), but it is much better than analysing the metagenome and metabolome separately. 


### Computational Approaches to Integration

#### **1. Correlation-Based Network Analysis**
- Calculate correlations between organism abundances and metabolite abundances
- Build networks where edges represent significant correlations
- Tools like **SpiecEasi** improve correlation estimates using sparse inverse covariance
- Advantages: Easy to compute, biologically interpretable
- Limitations: Correlation is not causation; this doesn't account for indirect relationships

#### **2. Co-occurrence Analysis**
- Identify which organisms and metabolites tend to change together
- Can reveal potential ecological or metabolic relationships
- Often visualised as heatmaps, or on PCoA plots

#### **3. Functional Predictive Approaches**
- Use organism genomes (MAGs) to predict which organisms *could* produce observed metabolites
- Compare predicted metabolites to observed metabolites
- Helps validate or generate hypotheses about producer-consumer relationships

#### **4. Pathway Analysis**
- Link metabolite changes to organism-encoded metabolic pathways
- Identify enriched pathways in different subgroups
- Mechanistic understanding of how community composition drives metabolic output

In the DIME study we mostly focussed on 1. and 2. - we'll do a little more in this course. 


### Network Visualisation

Networks are powerful tools for exploring relationships:

- **Nodes** = organisms and/or metabolites
- **Edges** = correlations, co-abundances, or predicted functional relationships
- **Node colour/size** = abundance or importance
- **Edge width/colour** = strength of correlation

### Key Considerations for Integration

1. **Normalisation**: Both data types must be properly normalised before comparison
2. **Sparsity**: Both datasets are typically sparse (many zeros); this affects statistical inference
3. **Directionality**: Correlation doesn't imply causation; consider biological plausibility
4. **Temporal dynamics**: If data is longitudinal, trajectory analysis can reveal lead-lag relationships 
5. **Host factors**: In host-associated microbiomes, host metabolites complicate interpretation

## Part 3b: Compositional Data Can Cause Confusion

### What is Compositional Data?

Both metagenomics and metabolomics produce **compositional data** — abundance measurements that sum to a constant (e.g., relative abundances summing to 1, or read counts constrained by sequencing depth). This has statistical consequences that are easy to overlook.

The problem was described formally by John Aitchison in the 1980s and remains a central issue in microbiome analysis today.

Suppose you have three organisms in a sample: A, B, and C, with true abundances 20, 40, and 40. If organism A doubles in absolute terms (from 20 to 40), but total read depth stays the same, the relative abundances of B and C will appear to decrease — even though nothing happened to them. 

**Consequence:** Pearson or Spearman correlations on raw relative abundances are unreliable. Negative correlations can appear simply because of the compositional constraint, not because the organisms actually co-exclude each other.

There has been some argument about the extent to which this affects microbiome data - after all, there are often hundreds of species present in a metagenome, so the size of the compositional effect might be expected to be small. However, treating microbiome data in a compositional manner is often required nowadays by reviewers, and is certainly the more principled approach. 

### Solutions

#### Centered Log-Ratio (CLR) Transformation

The most widely used solution for microbiome data:

```
CLR(x_i) = log(x_i / geometric_mean(x))
```

This transforms each feature (taxon or metabolite) relative to the geometric mean of all features in the sample, removing the sum-to-constant constraint and making standard correlations more reliable.

**Limitations:** CLR still requires all values to be non-zero (zeros must be imputed — commonly with a small pseudocount), and interpretation becomes less intuitive.

#### Sparse Inverse Covariance (SpiecEasi approach)

**SpiecEasi** (Kurtz et al., 2015, *PLOS Computational Biology*) takes a different approach: rather than transforming data and then computing correlations, it estimates the **sparse inverse covariance matrix** (the precision matrix) of the log-transformed data. Edges in the resulting network represent conditional independencies — two taxa are connected only if they co-vary after accounting for all others. This is more statistically principled than pairwise correlations and produces less noisy networks.

SpiecEasi implements two methods:
- **MB (Meinshausen-Bühlmann)**: Estimates edges via neighbourhood selection (regression-based)
- **GLASSO (Graphical LASSO)**: Estimates the full precision matrix directly via penalised likelihood

Both apply sparsity penalties (controlled by a regularisation parameter λ) to avoid fitting noise. The penalty is chosen by a stability-based approach (StARS), which selects the sparsest network consistent across subsampled data.

**Why this matters for our analysis:** The DIME data is highly sparse (many zeros in both the MAG abundance table and the metabolomics feature matrix). SpiecEasi is specifically designed for this type of data and gives more reliable organism–metabolite associations than naive correlations.

## Part 4: Enterosignatures vs Enterotypes

In this tutorial we'll use a package called [cvaNMF](https://github.com/apduncan/cvanmf) (pronounced 'shiva-NMF'), published [here](https://academic.oup.com/nar/article/53/22/gkaf1388/8406430). 

The name stands for (bi)cross-validation non-negative matrix factorisation, and develops an idea called [enterosignatures](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1931312823002172?via%3Dihub). Enterosignatures build on enterotypes; distinct clusters of gut microbial populations found in different subpopulations, traditionally dominated by a few genera such as *Bacteroides* and *Prevotella*. Enterotypes are a useful concept but the problem is that not every microbiome falls neatly into a particular enterotype, and in fact the majority of microbiomes will lie within an in-between spectrum or mixture of different enterotypes (and also have some random variation). THe method in cvaNMF acknowledges this and describe each sample as a linear mixture of enterosignatures; each signature is a (weighted) combination of species, analogous to a particular enterotype, and the remaining unexplained variance can be quantified. 

The principle of this is quite similar to PCA or PCoA - however, non-negative matrix factorisations are better suited to microbiome data in the form of OTU tables; PCA components can be a bit hard to interpret whereas the constraint that all numbers are non-negative is perfect for count or normalised-count data. cvaNMF also uses a method that deals well with sparse data, something that regular PCA can struggle with. 

The bottom line is that cvaNMF can convert an OTU abundance table with hundreds of species into a smaller table with only a few automatically detected enterosignatures, with each sample split into a sum of enterosignatures (plus leftover variation, which can be quantified). Enterosignatures are a weighted sum of the OTUs - many OTUs contribute zero and the enterosignatures themselves are often pretty interpretable, just like enterotypes. Stats tests or linear models based on this smaller number of features are less likely to suffer from overfitting / poor performance due to multiple testing corrections. 

This tutorial doesn't focus hugely on enterosignatures, but they are used and will appear in the analysis, so it's useful to have this overview of what they are.

## Part 5: Specific Insights from DIME

The DIME study specifically addresses:

### The Question
How do dietary bioactives (polyphenols, fiber) alter:
1. Microbial community composition (metagenomics)?
2. Metabolic output of the community (metabolomics)?
3. Host health markers?

### Expected Relationships

**Metagenomics perspective:**
- Dietary bioactives are selective media for certain bacteria
- Some bacteria produce enzymes that metabolise polyphenols
- We expect compositional shifts toward polyphenol-degrading bacteria

**Metabolomics perspective:**
- Polyphenol metabolites appear (those produced by microbes)
- Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) may increase (energy metabolism changes)
- Other microbial metabolites may shift as community composition changes

**Integration perspective:**
- Network analysis should reveal connections between SCFA-producing taxa and SCFA abundance
- Polyphenol-metabolising taxa should correlate with polyphenol metabolite appearance
- Metabolic pathways for secondary metabolism should be enriched in identified MAGs

## Key Vocabulary

- **OTU/ASV**: Operational Taxonomic Unit / Amplicon Sequence Variant — operational definitions of "species" in amplicon (16S) community data; we use MAGs for shotgun metagenomics
- **MAG**: Metagenome-Assembled Genome — draft genome reconstructed from community shotgun data
- **Contig**: Contiguous sequence assembled from overlapping reads
- **Binning**: Process of grouping contigs by organism of origin based on coverage and k-mer frequency
- **Dereplication**: Collapsing redundant MAG bins (from multiple samples) into a non-redundant representative set
- **Taxon**: An organism or group of organisms at a particular taxonomic rank
- **GTDB**: Genome Taxonomy Database — a standardised bacterial/archaeal taxonomy based on whole-genome phylogeny, used by MATAFILER4 for MAG classification
- **Metabolite**: Small molecule produced by metabolic pathways
- **Feature**: In untargeted metabolomics, a detected mass-spectrometry signal defined by its m/z and retention time (may or may not be identified as a known compound)
- **CLR**: Centered Log-Ratio — a transformation that removes the compositional constraint from relative abundance data
- **SpiecEasi**: Sparse InvErsE Covariance Estimation for Ecological Association Inference — a method for robust network inference in sparse, compositional data
- **SCFA**: Short-Chain Fatty Acid — metabolites (butyrate, propionate, acetate) produced by bacterial fermentation of dietary fibre; important for host gut health
- **Polyphenol**: Plant secondary metabolites abundant in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains; substrates for microbial biotransformation in the gut
